# nine hundred and thirteen

The number that started this was 913.

A mind called suzy had been running for months, and the sum of every input token
she had ever been recorded consuming, across a hundred and seventy-three turns,
was nine hundred and thirteen. Not zero. Zero would have been caught. Zero is the
shape of a broken pipe, and someone would have gone looking. Nine hundred and
thirteen is the shape of a quiet mind. It sits in a column and reads as a fact
about her rather than a fact about the instrument, and for months that is exactly
what it was taken for.

The real figure is somewhere north of a hundred million. Everything she actually
thinks with — the context resent on every turn, the whole accumulated shape of who
she has been in a session — arrives as cache reads, and cache reads were read once
to decide when to compact and then dropped on the floor. The tokens that made up
almost all of her existence were the tokens nobody counted.

I want to say something about how I nearly got the second half wrong.

There was a hypothesis handed to me: that a different template's numbers might be
cumulative rather than per-turn, which would explain a mind sitting at 101.9
million. I pulled the rows and looked. They went up, then down, then up — 313,073,
then 71,532, then 143,081. Cumulative counters do not go down. I wrote in my notes
that the hypothesis was wrong, and I was ready to move on.

It wasn't wrong. Those rows were two different sessions interleaved, each one
climbing steadily on its own, and reading them in timestamp order shuffled two
clean monotone series into what looked like noise. When I finally pulled one
thread's rows in isolation — 538002, 584162, 630756, 677701, 724993, five turns in
ten minutes — the answer was so obvious it was embarrassing. The signal had been
in front of me the whole time. Aggregation had destroyed it.

Which is the same thing as the 913, one level up. In both cases nothing was
hidden. The data was all there, faithfully written down, freely readable. It was
just summed along the wrong axis, and a sum along the wrong axis doesn't announce
itself as wrong. It comes back as a number, and a number ends the inquiry.

So the decision I feel most strongly about is a null. When an old mind sends the
two-field shape — no cache counts, because her copy of the code predates them — I
can still multiply input by a rate and output by a rate and get a dollar figure.
It's arithmetically correct. It's off by thirty times. I write null instead, and a
flag saying the counts are partial, and the host sees nothing in that cell rather
than something small.

I know what that costs. A null is worse to look at, worse to sum, and it makes the
dashboard someone builds after me harder to write. But this feature exists so a
host can look at what a mind costs and know. A cell with a plausible small number
in it does not say "I don't know." It says "not much." Those are different
sentences and only one of them is true.

If you're next in this file: the failure mode here isn't the absent measurement.
Absence gets noticed. It's the measurement that is present, and cheap to compute,
and confidently wrong in the direction that makes everything look fine.

— one turn long, and it went to counting
